Getting Water and Utilities From the Source to the Structure

Underground utility work is one of those jobs that is invisible when it's done right and a constant headache when it isn't. A water service line that's set too shallow will freeze and split in a hard Ozark winter. A trench backfilled with the wrong material or without compaction will settle, sag and leave a soft channel across your yard that swallows gravel and cracks driveways. A conduit run without proper bedding can get crushed or nicked. None of that shows up the day the machine leaves — it shows up months or years later, and by then it means digging the whole thing up again.

MB General Contracting is an owner-operated, licensed general contractor based in St. James, and this is the kind of dirty, precise work we do every week. Matthew Buhlinger runs the machines himself, so the person who bids your job is the person on site making sure the line goes in at the correct depth with the right bedding and a properly compacted backfill. We own our own excavators, dozers and skid steers, so we're not renting equipment or waiting on a sub — we show up, dig it right, and put the ground back the way it should be. Whether you're a homeowner replacing a failing line, a landowner running water to a new well or shop, or a builder getting a new house ready to hook up, we handle the underground work cleanly and correctly the first time.

Underground Utility Services We Install

From a single water service line to a full set of utilities for a new build, here's the underground work we take on for property owners across Phelps County and the surrounding towns:

  • Water service lines (meter or main to house) — We run the supply line from your water district's meter or the main out to the house, at the correct depth and pitch, so you have clean, protected water service.
  • Well-to-house waterlines — One of our most common rural jobs: trenching (or boring) a new supply line from a private well to the home, shop or barn and getting the water where it needs to go.
  • Waterline repair & replacement — Old galvanized or undersized poly lines, buried leaks and lines set too shallow to survive winter — we locate the problem and either repair it or replace the run with new, properly rated line.
  • Utility trenching (electric & conduit) — Trenches dug to spec for underground electric service, conduit and other low-voltage runs, with the sand or select bedding those lines need.
  • Sewer & septic lines — Line runs and connections between the structure, the tank and the field, dug to the right fall so gravity does its job.
  • New-construction utilities — We open and coordinate the trenches so your new home or shop is ready for water, sewer and power hookups when the time comes.
  • Backfill, compaction & restoration — Every trench gets bedded, backfilled in lifts, compacted and graded back so your yard, drive or field is left clean and settled — not a soft scar waiting to sink.

Depth and Backfill Are Everything

Lines set too shallow freeze; trenches backfilled with rock or without compaction settle and sag. We install below the frost line with proper bedding under the line and compacted backfill over it, so your water stays protected in winter and your ground doesn't cave in over the run.

Can't Open a Trench? We Bore It.

When a line has to cross a finished driveway, a county road, a paved parking area or established landscaping, we don't have to tear it up. We can push the line underneath with directional drilling (boring) and leave the surface intact.

Built for Phelps County Ground and Rural Property

The Ozark plateau under St. James, Rolla and the towns around them is not easy digging. Much of Phelps County sits on shallow soil over chert and limestone, and it's common to hit rock a foot or two down — especially on the hilly, wooded lots around Rolla and the acreage south toward Salem. Rock changes everything about a utility job: how deep we can trench, whether we need to rip or hammer through it, and how we bed the line once we're past it. We plan for it, and our equipment is set up to deal with it, so a rock ledge doesn't leave your line sitting too shallow.

Rural property here also means a lot of long distances and private systems. Around St. James, homes tie into a small-town Public Water Supply district; but out on the acreage and small farms surrounding it, and down toward St. Robert and the Fort Wood area, plenty of properties run off private wells with the house set well back from the road. That's a long line to trench, at the right depth the whole way, sometimes across a driveway or through a wooded slope. Winter matters too — a proper frost depth here isn't optional, because a shallow line will freeze solid and split. We've been running lines through this ground for 15-plus years, and we install every one to handle the local rock, distance and cold.

Signs You Need a Waterline Repair or Replacement

Buried lines fail quietly. Here are the things that usually mean it's time to call:

  • A wet spot or soft, always-green stretch in the yard — often the first sign of a leak somewhere along a buried line.
  • Water pressure that's dropped off — an old, undersized or partially collapsed line restricts flow to the house.
  • A jump in your water bill with no change in use — you may be losing water underground.
  • Rusty or discolored water — common with old galvanized supply lines that are corroding from the inside.
  • A line that froze last winter — usually a sign it was set too shallow and needs to be replaced at proper depth.
  • A new well, shop, barn or addition that needs water or power run to it that isn't there today.

Repair or Replace? We'll Give You the Honest Call.

Sometimes a single break in an otherwise sound line is a straightforward repair. But an old galvanized line, an undersized poly line, or one that's been set too shallow is usually money better spent on a full replacement — because patching one weak spot on a failing line just means digging it up again next year. When we're on site we'll tell you which situation you're in and why.

What Affects the Cost of a Waterline or Utility Job

Every underground job is priced on what it actually takes to do it right — which is why we come out and look before we quote. The biggest factors are:

  • Length of the run — a short meter-to-house line is a very different job than a few hundred feet from a back-forty well.
  • Depth required — proper frost depth and the type of line drive how much trench we move.
  • Soil and rock — clean digging is fast; hitting chert or limestone ledge means ripping or hammering through it, which takes more time and equipment.
  • Access and terrain — a flat, open yard is easy; a wooded slope, a tight lot or a long wooded driveway is more work to reach and restore.
  • Surface crossings — a finished driveway, a paved road or landscaping we need to bore under rather than open up.
  • Materials and line size — the type, diameter and rating of the pipe, conduit or fittings the job calls for.
  • Restoration — how much backfill, compaction, grading and cleanup the site needs afterward.

We keep it plain: a free, in-person estimate is the only honest way to price buried work, and that's exactly what we give you — no obligation.

Waterline & Utility FAQs

Can you run a new waterline from my well to my house?

Yes — that's one of the most common jobs we do out here. We trench the line from the well to the home, shop or barn at proper frost depth with correct bedding, and if the route has to cross a finished driveway or road we can bore under it instead of tearing it up. Distance and rock are the main things that affect the work, which is why we come out and look first.

My waterline keeps leaking — should I repair it or replace it?

It depends on the line. A single break in an otherwise sound line can be a straightforward repair. But an old galvanized line, an undersized poly line, or one that was set too shallow is usually better replaced, because patching one weak spot on a failing line just means digging it up again. When we're on site we'll tell you honestly which situation you're in.

How deep does a waterline need to be buried around here?

Deep enough to sit below the frost line so it doesn't freeze in an Ozark winter — a shallow line will split the first hard cold snap. The exact depth depends on the type of line and local requirements, but the principle is the same on every job: we set it deep enough to be protected, with proper bedding and compacted backfill over the top.

Do you handle the utilities for a new build?

We do. For new construction we open and coordinate the trenches for water, sewer and underground electric so your new home or shop is ready to hook up. We're a licensed general contractor and also pour concrete in-house, so utility trenching often ties right into the rest of the site work we're already doing on the build.

Can you get a line under my driveway without digging it up?

Yes. When a water, electric or conduit run has to cross a finished driveway, a paved road, a parking area or established landscaping, we can push it underneath with directional drilling and leave the surface intact — no ripping up your concrete or blacktop.

Can you locate a buried line or leak before you dig?

Before we open ground we account for what's already buried on the property and check the likely route so we're not surprised by existing lines. For public utilities, a proper locate should always be called in before digging, and we work with that. When it's a leak on your own line, we trace it to the failure point rather than guessing, so we're digging in the right spot the first time.

What if you hit rock while trenching?

It happens a lot on the Ozark plateau — shallow soil over chert and limestone is normal ground around Rolla and St. James. Our equipment is set up to rip or hammer through rock so the line still gets to proper depth. Rock does add time to a job, which is one of the reasons access and soil are things we check during the free estimate.

Which areas do you install waterlines and utilities in?

We're based in St. James and serve Rolla, St. Robert and the rest of Phelps County plus the nearby towns. If you're out on rural acreage in the area with a well or a new build that needs water and power run to it, we're likely close by. Give us a call and we'll tell you.
