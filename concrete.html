Concrete on Phelps County Ground

Pouring good concrete around here isn't the same as pouring it on flat, sandy ground somewhere else. This is the Ozark plateau — hilly, and full of chert and rock. In Rolla especially, you'll hit rock ledges and cherty clay that have to be dealt with before you form anything; sometimes that means extra digging, sometimes it means bringing in and compacting proper base rock. Either way, our excavators and skid steers are already on the trailer, so we handle it in one shot instead of stopping the job to call somebody.

Missouri weather is the other half of it. We go through real freeze-thaw cycles out here, and water that gets under a slab and freezes is what lifts and cracks concrete over the winters. That's why drainage and slope get designed into the pour from the start — the pad has to shed water away from the house and off the surface, not trap it. On the rural acreage around St. James, Newburg and Salem, we're also pouring for long approaches, shop pads and barn slabs on ground that isn't level to begin with, which is exactly the kind of site prep our equipment is built for. Local ground, local weather, poured by people who work in it every week.

Signs It's Time for New Concrete

Concrete gives you warning before it fails outright. If you're seeing any of these, it's worth a free look:

  • Cracks you can catch a boot heel on — hairline cracks are normal; wide, offset or spreading cracks mean the base moved
  • Sections that have sunk or heaved — uneven slabs are a trip hazard and usually a base or drainage problem underneath
  • Water ponding on the surface — standing water means the slope is wrong and freeze-thaw damage is coming
  • Pitting, flaking or crumbling — a surface that's spalling apart won't get better on its own
  • A gravel drive you're tired of grading — if you're constantly re-rocking and regrading, a poured drive ends the cycle
  • A shop or garage on dirt — a real slab makes the building usable, cleaner and worth more

What Affects the Cost

Every pour is priced off the actual site — there's no honest flat rate for concrete. When Matthew comes out for your free estimate, these are the things that move the number:

  • Size and thickness — square footage plus how thick it needs to be (a car drive, a truck apron and an equipment pad are not the same slab)
  • Site access — how easy it is to get a truck and equipment to the pour, and how far the mud has to travel
  • Soil and rock — chert, rock ledges or soft clay change how much digging and base work the ground needs
  • Base and drainage work — how much excavation, fill, compaction and slope the site takes to do it right
  • Tear-out — whether there's old concrete to demo and haul off first
  • Reinforcement and finish — rebar vs. wire, and a plain broom finish vs. something dressier
  • Distance from our St. James base — travel to the far side of the county or beyond

Concrete FAQs

Do you actually pour the concrete yourselves, or sub it out?

We pour it ourselves. Concrete is one of our in-house trades — the same MB crew that preps and grades your base forms and finishes the pour. You never get handed off to a subcontractor, and you've got one company accountable for the whole job.

Can you tear out my old driveway and replace it?

Yes. We'll demo the old concrete, haul it off, re-prep and compact the base, reset forms to the right slope, and pour a new driveway or patio in its place — all with our own equipment and crew.

Will my new concrete crack?

All concrete moves and every slab will eventually show some hairline cracking — that's normal and not a defect. What prevents the bad cracks (the wide, offset, heaving kind) is a properly compacted, well-draining base and control joints placed correctly. Because we handle the base ourselves, we control the biggest factor in whether concrete stays sound.

What are control joints and do I need them?

Control joints are the lines you see cut or tooled into a slab. They give the concrete a planned place to crack as it shrinks and moves, instead of cracking wherever it wants. We lay them out at the right spacing for the size and shape of your pour — they're a standard part of doing the job right, not an upgrade.

How long before I can use my new concrete?

You can usually walk on it the next day. We generally recommend staying off it with vehicles for about a week so it can cure and gain strength — concrete keeps hardening for weeks after that. We'll give you the specifics for your pour and the weather when we finish, so you know exactly when to drive on it.

Why does base prep matter so much?

Because a slab is only as good as what's under it. Concrete doesn't crack and settle because the concrete was bad — it fails because the base was soft, uneven, or held water that froze. We strip, grade and compact the base and build in slope to drain it before we ever pour. That prep is the whole reason our concrete lasts, and it's why we insist on doing the dirt work ourselves.

Do you pour shop floors and slabs for outbuildings?

We do — garage floors, shop floors, shed and barn pads, addition slabs and equipment pads are a big part of what we pour. We'll cut and level the pad, set the base, and finish the floor flat and hard enough to take vehicles, tools and traffic.
