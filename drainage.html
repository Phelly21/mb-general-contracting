What We Do — Drainage & Erosion Services

Drainage isn't one product; it's picking the right tool for the water problem in front of you. Here's the range of work we handle, and what each piece is actually for:

  • French drains — a gravel-packed, perforated pipe set below grade that quietly pulls subsurface and seep water away from low spots, wet yards and foundation walls before it can pool or push against the house.
  • Yard & surface drainage and swales — regrading soggy areas and cutting shallow, shaped swales so surface water sheets away instead of sitting on clay that refuses to drink it.
  • Downspout & gutter lines — buried solid pipe that takes roof water from your gutters and dumps it well downhill of the foundation, instead of blasting it into the flowerbed six inches from the wall.
  • Culverts & ditching — sized and set culvert pipe under driveways plus cleaned or re-cut roadside ditches so runoff keeps moving and your access doesn't wash out.
  • Foundation & basement drainage — re-establishing positive grade away from the house, adding perimeter and footing drains, and routing everything to daylight so the basement and crawlspace stay dry.
  • Erosion control — armoring the ground that's washing with rock, riprap, proper grade and vegetation-ready shaping so slopes, drives and disturbed dirt stop moving downhill.
  • Runoff & site grading — reshaping how an entire site collects and releases water so it flows in a controlled path to a place that can handle it, not toward your neighbor or your own foundation.
  • Detention & retention shaping — excavating and grading basins that hold and slowly release water on larger or steeper properties where runoff needs somewhere to go.

Local Ground: Why Drainage Is Different in the Ozarks

Drainage advice from a flat, sandy part of the country doesn't translate to Phelps County. Two things drive nearly every water problem we see here. First, the soil: a lot of our ground is heavy clay with a thin layer of topsoil, and clay does not absorb water well. During a downpour it sheds almost everything at the surface, and once it's saturated it stays wet for days — which is exactly why a "low spot" turns into a pond that lingers long after the rain stops.

Second, the terrain. This is rolling Ozark-plateau country — hills, slopes and creek bottoms, with plenty of chert and rock not far under the surface. Water moves fast down a slope and collects hard at the bottom, so a house or driveway sitting mid-slope catches everything coming from above. In and around Rolla you'll hit rock quickly on the hilly ground and in the newer edge subdivisions where the natural grade got cut and filled during construction. Here in St. James and out on the surrounding rural acreage, long gravel drives, farm ground and older homes without modern foundation drainage are the common culprits. Down toward Waynesville and the Roubidoux, the bluffy terrain and creek-bottom flats make grading and drainage the difference between a usable yard and a swamp. Knowing which of these you're dealing with — clay that won't drain, rock you can't dig through cheaply, or a slope funneling water at you — is half the job, and it's why we walk the ground before we ever quote a fix.

Signs You Have a Drainage Problem

Water rarely announces itself politely. If you're seeing any of these, the grade or flow on your property needs attention:

  • Standing water that lingers — puddles or soggy ground that stick around a day or more after the rain has stopped.
  • A wet or damp basement or crawlspace — water seeping in, musty air, or efflorescence (white chalky residue) on the walls.
  • Water pooling against the foundation — flowerbeds or ground that slope toward the house instead of away from it.
  • Eroding or rutted slopes and driveways — gullies, exposed roots, or gravel that keeps washing to the bottom of the drive after every storm.
  • A driveway that floods or a culvert that backs up — water crossing your access instead of passing under it.
  • Dead grass, moss or mosquito-breeding low spots — the yard staying wet enough to kill turf or grow what shouldn't be there.
  • Mud tracked into the house — soft, saturated ground near doors and walkways that never fully dries out.

Catch these early and the fix is usually straightforward. Ignore them and water works its way into foundations, footings and slabs where the repair gets a lot bigger.

What Affects the Cost of Drainage Work

Every property drains differently, so we quote after we've seen it. That said, these are the factors that move a drainage job up or down in price:

  • The size and length of the run — how much pipe, trench and regrading the fix requires to move water the full distance to a safe outlet.
  • Soil and rock — clean digging in soil goes faster than fighting chert and rock, which is common on the hilly ground around Rolla and Phelps County.
  • Site access — whether our equipment can reach the work area, or the job is tight, fenced or landscaped and has to be done carefully.
  • Where the water can go — a nearby ditch or slope to daylight is simple; carrying water a long way or to a basin adds work.
  • The type of system — a surface swale is one thing; a full French drain, footing drain or culvert install is another.
  • Materials — pipe size, gravel, fabric, rock and riprap, and any concrete or catch basins the design needs.
  • Restoration — how much grading, seeding or flatwork it takes to put the surface back the way you want it.

We'll always talk through the trade-offs on site so you understand what's driving the number and where there's a simpler option.

Drainage FAQs

Water pools in my yard after every rain — what fixes that?

Usually a combination of regrading the low areas and adding a swale or French drain to carry the water out to a safe outlet. On clay ground the soil won't soak it up, so the answer is almost always giving the water a shaped path to leave rather than trying to make it drain straight down. We'll pinpoint the cause on site and recommend the simplest fix that actually works.

How do I keep water out of my basement or away from my foundation?

It starts with grade: the ground for the first several feet around the house needs to slope away from it, not toward it. From there we add downspout lines that carry roof water well clear of the wall, and where needed a perimeter or footing drain that routes seep water to daylight. Fixing the grade and the roof water solves the majority of wet-basement calls we get.

Can you stop erosion on my slope or driveway?

Yes. We control erosion by correcting the grade so water isn't allowed to run in a concentrated stream, then armoring the ground with rock or riprap and drainage where it's needed. On steeper ground a retaining wall may be the right call to hold the slope, and a properly crowned and ditched driveway grade keeps a gravel drive from washing out.

French drain or surface drainage — which do I need?

It depends on where the water is. Surface drainage — regrading and swales — is for water sitting on top of the ground. A French drain is for subsurface or seep water and for pulling water away from foundations and persistent wet spots below grade. Many yards need a bit of both, and we'll tell you honestly on site which one your problem calls for instead of overbuilding.

Do I need a culvert for my driveway?

If your drive crosses a ditch or the natural path water takes along the road, yes — a correctly sized culvert lets that water pass under the drive instead of over it or backing up behind it. We size and set the pipe and re-cut the ditch so runoff keeps moving and your access stays passable. It often gets handled together with driveway grading.

My property is mostly clay and rock — will drainage even work?

It will, but the design has to respect the ground. Because our clay doesn't absorb water, we lean on surface grading, swales and piped runs that carry water off to daylight rather than expecting it to soak in. Rock affects how we dig and route the trench. This is exactly the kind of Ozark ground we work in every week, so we design around it rather than fighting it.

Can you do the drainage while you're already doing other site work?

That's the ideal way to do it. If we're on site for excavating, a new drive or a wall, building drainage into the same job is more efficient and makes sure everything is set up to shed water correctly from the start. One crew, one mobilization, one grade that all works together.
