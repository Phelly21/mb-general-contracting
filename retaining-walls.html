A retaining wall does two jobs at once. It holds back soil that wants to move downhill, and it turns a steep, wasted bank into flat, usable ground — a level yard, a wider driveway, a garden bed, or a spot for the shop or shed. Around Phelps County that second job matters as much as the first, because so much of the property here sits on a grade. If you've got a hillside eating into your yard, a bank sliding toward the driveway, or a spot where nothing will hold and the rain keeps carrying the dirt away, a properly built wall is usually the fix.

MB General Contracting is owner-operated by Matthew Buhlinger, a licensed general contractor who has worked this ground for 15+ years. We build retaining walls the way they have to be built to last on Ozark soil: excavate down to firm ground, set a compacted base, lay level courses of block or place boulders with a backward batter, backfill with drainage rock, and run water away from behind the wall. Because we own our own excavators, dozers and skid steers, we handle the digging, the wall and the grading around it as one job — you're not juggling separate crews.

Drainage Behind the Wall

The number-one reason retaining walls lean, bulge and fail is water pressure building up behind them. Every wall we build gets a compacted base, free-draining backfill rock and a drain line that carries groundwater out before it can push on the wall. On our clay-and-chert soils, that drainage is the difference-maker — not an add-on.

Built For Phelps County Ground

Why Walls Matter On Ozark Terrain

The land around St. James, Rolla and out toward Waynesville sits on the Ozark plateau — rolling, hilly ground with chert, clay and shallow rock never far below the surface. That terrain gives us beautiful properties, but it also means a lot of homes and shops are wedged into a grade, with a bank on one side and a drop on the other. When it rains hard, water runs downhill fast, and unprotected soil goes with it.

That combination is exactly why retaining walls are one of the most common jobs we do here. Rolla's newer subdivisions on the edges of town are often cut into hillsides, leaving steep back-lot banks that homeowners want to tame into usable yard. Older in-town lots and rural acreage around St. James have long, sloping driveways and creek-side ground where erosion is a constant. And clay soil holds water — so a wall built without drainage on this ground is a wall that will eventually lean. We build for the soil that's actually here, not for a flat lot in the city.

Signs You Need A Retaining Wall

Most retaining wall calls start with one of these problems. If any of them sound like your property, it's worth a free look:

  • A slope you can't use — a bank too steep to mow, plant or walk on that's eating into your yard.
  • Soil washing away — rain is cutting gullies, carrying mulch and dirt downhill, or exposing roots and foundations.
  • An old wall leaning or bulging — timbers rotting, blocks tipping forward, or a stone wall belling out at the middle.
  • A bank creeping toward something — a hillside moving toward the house, driveway, shop or septic field.
  • A narrow, dangerous driveway — no room to turn around or pass because a bank crowds one side.
  • Standing water or a soft, sliding spot — ground that stays wet and won't hold, often a drainage problem behind a slope.

When It's Really A Drainage Problem

A leaning wall or a sliding bank is often a water problem wearing a soil costume. During the estimate we look at where the water comes from and where it needs to go — sometimes the right answer pairs a wall with a drainage system or regraded driveway so the fix actually lasts.

What Affects The Cost Of A Retaining Wall

Every wall is priced to the site, so an in-person look is the only honest way to quote one. These are the factors that move the price up or down:

  • Height and length of the wall — taller and longer walls need more material, and height changes the base and reinforcement required.
  • Material — segmental block, natural boulders and timber differ in cost and in how much labor they take to place.
  • Soil and rock — clay, loose fill or shallow rock all change how much excavation and base work is needed.
  • Drainage required — how much water sits behind the wall determines the backfill and drain work to do it right.
  • Site access — how easily equipment and material can reach the wall on a tight, sloped or wooded lot.
  • Excavation and grading — the amount of digging, hauling and finish grading around the wall.
  • Curves, corners and steps — tiers, terraces and turns add layout and cutting time.

We'll walk all of this with you on-site and give you a straight, itemized estimate — no pressure, no surprises.

Retaining Wall FAQs

Block or boulder — which is better?

Both hold well; it comes down to look, height and budget. Boulder and natural stone walls suit rural, wooded and acreage properties and blend into the landscape. Segmental block gives a cleaner, uniform, finished look that fits homes and subdivisions and comes in a range of colors. We'll help you pick the right one for your site during the free estimate.

Why do retaining walls lean, bulge or fail?

Almost always poor base or no drainage. On our clay-and-chert ground, water builds up behind a wall and pushes it out — and a wall set on soft or un-compacted ground settles unevenly. We prevent both: we excavate to firm ground, compact a crushed-rock base, batter the wall back into the slope, and backfill with drainage rock and a drain line so water leaves before it can push.

Can a wall give me more flat yard?

Yes — that's one of the best uses of a retaining wall. We cut into a slope and hold it back to create level, mowable, usable ground where there was only a bank. On a long or steep slope we may terrace it into two or more level tiers instead of one tall wall.

Why does drainage behind the wall matter so much?

Soil behind a wall soaks up rain and groundwater and gets heavy — that water pressure is the single biggest force trying to tip your wall over. By backfilling with free-draining rock and running a drain line, we relieve that pressure so the wall only has to hold soil, not water. It's the step cheap walls skip, and it's why they fail. See our drainage work for how we route that water safely away.

How tall can a retaining wall be?

Lower walls are straightforward. As height increases, a wall needs a deeper base, reinforcement and sometimes engineering, and taller projects are often better handled as terraced tiers. We'll assess the slope and soil on-site and recommend the safest, most cost-effective approach for the height you need.

Can you build a wall to widen my driveway or make a parking pad?

Yes. We hold back the high-side bank or build up the low side to gain width, a turnaround or a level parking pad — and because we also do driveway grading and concrete, we can finish the surface too. It's a common job on the narrow, sloped drives around Rolla and St. James.

Do you also fix or replace failing walls?

Yes. Old timber walls that have rotted and leaning block or stone walls can often be rebuilt right — this time on a proper base with real drainage. We'll look at what's there and tell you honestly whether it can be reset or needs replacing.
