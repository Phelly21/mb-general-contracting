Directional drilling — also called horizontal directional drilling (HDD) or trenchless boring — is a way to install an underground line without digging an open trench across the surface. Instead of trenching straight through a driveway, road or finished lawn, the machine bores a path beneath it and then pulls the line back through that path. The surface on top is left essentially the way we found it. It's the method to reach for whenever a waterline, electric run or conduit has to cross something you don't want torn up and rebuilt afterward.

Here in Phelps County a lot of properties are laid out so the well, meter, transformer or new building sits on the opposite side of a drive or road from where the line has to end up. Open trenching would mean cutting through concrete, gravel or established landscaping and then paying to restore all of it. Boring underneath avoids most of that. As a licensed, owner-operated contractor with 15+ years of local site work, we handle the boring and the connections as one job — no juggling separate crews.

How Directional Drilling Works

The idea is simple even if the equipment isn't. First, existing underground utilities are located and marked so we know what's already in the ground. Then the drill sends a bore head along a controlled, gradual path from an entry point, under the obstacle, to an exit point on the far side. Once that pilot path is open, the new line — pipe or conduit — is attached and pulled back through it. When it's done you have a line running cleanly underneath the driveway, road or yard, with only the small entry and exit spots to tidy up instead of a long open scar.

When Directional Drilling Makes Sense

  • Under driveways & parking — run a line across without cutting concrete, asphalt or gravel that you'd otherwise have to patch back in
  • Under roads & lanes — cross a public road, private drive or county lane without tearing it open and rebuilding it
  • Under finished landscaping — protect established lawns, gardens, mulch beds and hardscape that took years to grow in
  • Under sidewalks, patios & slabs — get to the other side of a poured surface without breaking it up
  • Waterlines — bore the crossing for a new or replacement water service line and pull the pipe through
  • Electrical & utility conduit — run conduit for power, low-voltage or other utility lines under the surface
  • Where open trenching isn't practical — tight spots, sensitive surfaces and places a trench simply can't go without a big mess

Less Damage, Less Restoration

Because there's no long open trench across the surface, boring means far less disturbance up top — and far less to repair when it's finished. Your driveway, road or yard stays intact instead of becoming a second project. On a lot of crossings, the restoration you avoid is the whole reason boring wins.

Trenchless Crossings on Phelps County Ground

Boring on Ozark-plateau ground is not the same as boring in flat, sandy soil. Around Rolla and St. James the subsurface runs to clay, chert and rock, and hitting rock changes how a bore goes — how fast, how the head steers and how the path has to be planned. That's exactly why locating and a realistic look at the ground come first. We've spent 15+ years working this soil with our own excavators and equipment, so we know what's typical here and plan the crossing around it rather than being surprised by it.

The layout of local properties is the other half of the story. Many rural parcels around St. James and Rolla have a long drive off a county road, a well or shared line on one side, and the house or new build on the other. Newer subdivisions on the edges of Rolla put finished landscaping, sidewalks and poured drives right where a new service line needs to cross. In both cases boring gets the line to the far side without turning the approach or the yard into a trench.

We handle these crossings for homeowners, builders and property owners throughout St. James, Rolla and the surrounding Phelps County towns. If you're not sure whether boring or trenching fits your site, that's a normal question — we'll look at it in person and tell you straight.

When Boring Beats Digging

Directional drilling isn't the right call for every line, but there are clear situations where it's the obvious one:

  • A finished driveway is in the way — you don't want to saw-cut concrete or dig up gravel you just had put down
  • The line has to cross a road — trenching a road means closing it, cutting it and rebuilding it; boring goes under it
  • The landscaping is established — mature lawn, beds, trees and hardscape you don't want to lose to a trench
  • There's a patio, sidewalk or slab between the two points — poured surfaces that would be expensive to break and replace
  • Surface restoration would cost more than the crossing — when the repair bill is the real expense, boring often comes out ahead overall
  • Access is tight — spots where an open trench and the equipment to dig it just don't fit cleanly

What Affects the Cost of a Bore

Every crossing is different, so we quote each one after we've seen it. The things that move the price up or down are straightforward:

  • Distance of the crossing — how far the line has to travel from entry to exit
  • What's underneath — soil versus clay versus chert and rock; rock is slower and harder to bore through
  • What's being run — the size and type of waterline or conduit being pulled through, and the materials involved
  • Depth required — how deep the bore has to stay under the surface and any obstacles
  • Site access — how easily equipment can reach the entry and exit points
  • Tie-in work — how much connection, excavation or restoration is needed at each end

We'll walk you through which of these apply to your site so you understand the number, not just receive it. When you weigh boring against open trenching, remember to factor in the surface you won't have to tear out and rebuild — that's often where boring earns its keep.

Directional Drilling FAQs

What can you run with directional drilling?

Waterlines, electrical conduit and similar utility lines — anywhere you need to cross under a surface you'd rather not dig up. If you have a line that has to get to the other side of a driveway, road or finished yard, boring is usually an option worth looking at.

Is boring more expensive than trenching?

Per foot, boring can cost more than an open trench. But once you factor in not having to tear out and replace a driveway, road or landscaping, it often comes out ahead overall. On crossings where the restoration would be the biggest expense, boring frequently wins. We'll help you weigh both for your specific site.

Do you locate utilities before boring?

Yes. Existing underground lines are located and marked before we bore, and the bore path is planned around them. That's a basic part of doing the crossing safely, and we don't skip it.

How far can you bore under a driveway or road?

It depends on the crossing — the distance, the depth and what the ground is made of all factor in. Most residential driveway and road crossings around St. James and Rolla are well within range. The best way to know for your site is to have us come look; the free estimate covers exactly that.

Will boring damage my driveway or yard?

That's the whole point of trenchless boring — to avoid the long open trench that damages the surface. The line goes underneath, so instead of a scar across your driveway or lawn you're left with only the small entry and exit spots to tidy up. It's far less disturbance than open trenching.

Does rocky Ozark ground affect the bore?

It can. Around Phelps County the subsurface runs to clay, chert and rock, and hitting rock changes how a bore goes and how it's planned. We've worked this ground for 15+ years, so we plan for it up front rather than being caught off guard partway through.

Can you handle the waterline connection too, or just the bore?

We do both. Directional drilling ties directly into our waterline and utility work and excavating — we bore the crossing, pull the line, connect it and backfill, all with one crew.
